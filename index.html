01 Amount of Substance & The Mole

The **amount of substance** (\(n\)) is a standard SI chemical quantity measured in **moles (mol)**. Because atoms, molecules, and ions are too submicroscopic to count individually, the mole links microscopic particle numbers directly to measurable macroscopic masses.

The Standard Scale

Carbon-12 Standard

One mole is defined as the amount of substance containing exactly as many elementary entities as there are carbon atoms in exactly \(12\text{ g}\) of pure carbon-12 (\({}^{12}\text{C}\)).

\(1\text{ mol of Al} = 27.0\text{ g}\)
Avogadro's Constant (\(L\))

Number of Particles

The number of specified particles per mole of substance. It applies to atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, or formula units:

\(L = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}\)
Particle Count Equation

Converting Moles to Particles

The total number of particles (\(N\)) is found by multiplying moles (\(n\)) by Avogadro's constant (\(L\)):

\(N = n \times L\)
Molar Mass Relationships
\[\text{Moles } (n) = \frac{\text{Mass } (m)}{\text{Molar Mass } (M)} \quad \implies \quad m = n \times M\]

Where mass (\(m\)) is measured in grams (\(\text{g}\)), molar mass (\(M\) or \(M_r\)) in \(\text{g mol}^{-1}\), and amount of substance (\(n\)) in \(\text{mol}\).

02 Amount of Substance in Chemical Equations

Stoichiometric coefficients in a balanced chemical equation dictate the **mole ratios** between reacting particles and products. Two systematic approaches can be used to determine theoretical yield:

Method 1 • The Mole Ratio Route

Convert to Moles First

  1. Calculate moles of known reactant: \(n = \frac{m}{M}\).
  2. Apply the balanced equation stoichiometric ratio to deduce moles of unknown product.
  3. Convert product moles back into mass: \(m = n \times M\).
\(50.0\text{ g Na} \to n = \frac{50.0}{23.0} = 2.17\text{ mol}\)
\(\text{Ratio } 4\text{Na} : 2\text{Na}_2\text{O} = 2 : 1 \implies n(\text{Na}_2\text{O}) = 1.087\text{ mol}\)
\(m = 1.087 \times 62.0 = \mathbf{67.4\text{ g}}\)
Method 2 • Direct Reacting Mass Proportions

Direct Scaling by Total Formula Mass

  1. Multiply each formula mass by its stoichiometric balancing coefficient.
  2. Set up a direct mathematical cross-multiplication proportion between known mass and target product.
  3. Solve directly for mass \(m\).
\[4\text{Na} \to 2\text{Na}_2\text{O}\] \[(4 \times 23.0\text{ g}) \to (2 \times 62.0\text{ g}) \implies 92.0\text{ g} \to 124.0\text{ g}\] \[m = \frac{124.0 \times 50.0}{92.0} = \mathbf{67.4\text{ g}}\]

Crucible Dehydration Procedure & Data Pipeline

Laboratory Standard
1 Weigh Crucible & Lid

Dry clean crucible and lid. Cool in desiccator and record initial empty mass (\(25.68\text{ g}\)).

2 Add Hydrated Salt

Add crystals and reweigh (\(29.48\text{ g}\)). Salt mass = \(29.48 - 25.68 = 3.80\text{ g}\).

3 Heat Gently, Then Strongly

Heat with Bunsen burner using lid to avoid spitting until steam ceases.

4 Cool in Desiccator

Cool in desiccator so dry anhydrous salt does not absorb atmospheric moisture.

5 Heat to Constant Mass

Repeat heating, cooling, and reweighing until two successive masses agree (\(28.10\text{ g}\)).

Two Crucial Practical Assumptions (Examiner Favorites):
  • Assumption 1: All water has been lost. If heating is stopped prematurely or water remains trapped inside dense crystals, the calculated anhydrous mass will be too high, underestimating the mass of water and yielding an artificially low value for \(x\). Solution: Reheat to constant mass.
  • Assumption 2: No further thermal decomposition. Many hydrated transition salts (such as copper sulfate) decompose further at excessive temperatures into metal oxides and gaseous sulfur trioxide (\(\text{SO}_3\)). This causes excess mass loss, artificially inflating the apparent mass of water and yielding a value of \(x\) that is too high. Solution: Heat gently with a controlled flame.

04 Empirical & Molecular Formulae

Chemical formulae represent elemental composition in two complementary formats:

Empirical Formula

Simplest Whole-Number Ratio

The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element present in a compound.

Butane: \(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\)
Molecular Formula

Actual Number of Atoms

The actual number of atoms of each element present in one molecule of a compound:

\(\text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{Empirical Formula}) \times \frac{M_r}{\text{Empirical } M_r} \implies \text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}\)
Worked Example • Perfume Solvent

A sample contains \(0.60\text{ g}\) of carbon, \(0.15\text{ g}\) of hydrogen, and \(0.40\text{ g}\) of oxygen. Deduce its empirical formula:

Element Mass / g Moles (\(n = m/A_r\)) Divide by Smallest (\(0.025\)) Simplest Ratio
Carbon (\(\text{C}\)) \(0.60\) \(\frac{0.60}{12.0} = 0.050\) \(\frac{0.050}{0.025} = 2.0\) 2
Hydrogen (\(\text{H}\)) \(0.15\) \(\frac{0.15}{1.0} = 0.150\) \(\frac{0.150}{0.025} = 6.0\) 6
Oxygen (\(\text{O}\)) \(0.40\) \(\frac{0.40}{16.0} = 0.025\) \(\frac{0.025}{0.025} = 1.0\) 1
Empirical Formula: \(\text{C}_2\text{H}_6\text{O}\) (Ethanol or Dimethyl ether)
